\begin{abstract}
This report evaluates a teacher-reference 1DCNN--LSTM--ResNet, a proposed
1DCNN--LSTM--ResNet, and a tsai ResNet on the same processed Z24 dataset.
All experiments use 1,530 samples, 17 balanced classes, the same
setup-grouped train/validation/test split, and per-sensor Z-score normalization
calculated from the training set only. The teacher-reference pipeline preserves
the lecturer's sensor-first input convention and softmax recurrent activation,
whereas the proposed pipeline treats the 6,000 measurements as time steps,
uses a sigmoid recurrent activation, and adds Dropout, L2 regularization,
AdamW, \texttt{tf.data}, and two-GPU training. The tsai pipeline uses a
one-dimensional ResNet implemented with PyTorch/fastai and trains on one GPU.

The proposed pipeline increased test accuracy from 56.18\% to 60.00\% and
test macro F1 from 53.63\% to 55.02\%. Its train--test accuracy gap decreased
from 43.82 to 33.14 percentage points, indicating less severe overfitting.
However, the improvement was not uniform across classes and amounted to only
13 additional correct predictions among 340 test samples. The tsai ResNet
obtained 60.29\% test accuracy and 57.67\% macro F1. It therefore classified
only one more test sample correctly than the proposed LSTM, but improved macro
F1 by 2.65 percentage points, indicating a different class-level trade-off.
The proposed LSTM
also required 8.35 seconds per steady-state epoch, compared with 0.78 seconds
for the teacher-reference pipeline and 8.05 seconds for tsai. The main reason
for the LSTM cost is that it processes
approximately 3,000 recurrent steps instead of approximately 14. Finally, the
recorded benchmark policy was \texttt{float32}; therefore, this run does not
support a claim that mixed precision accelerated the proposed model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Objective and scope}

The objective is to determine how the complete pipeline and model changes
affected:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item classification performance on train, validation, and test data;
    \item overfitting and generalization to unseen setups;
    \item model size and training speed, including the tsai ResNet baseline;
    \item class-specific errors; and
    \item the validity of claims concerning GPU acceleration.
\end{enumerate}

The evaluated notebooks are:

\begin{lstlisting}
Proposed LSTM and tsai ResNet:
shm-ml-new.ipynb

Teacher-reference pipeline:
shm-old-lstm.ipynb
\end{lstlisting}

The first notebook contains two independent experiments: the proposed LSTM in
TensorFlow/Keras and the tsai ResNet in PyTorch/fastai. The teacher-reference
notebook is an adaptation of the lecturer's source code
to the processed dataset. It is not the unmodified original experiment. The
original code used pickle files, an input shape of \texttt{(5, 8000)}, and a
validation set containing training samples. For the benchmark reported here,
the teacher architecture and training style were retained, but all three pipelines
used the same processed data, grouped split, and training-only normalization.
This distinction is essential: the results below compare three trained models
on controlled data, not the new models against the accuracy originally reported
by the lecturer.

\section{Controlled dataset and evaluation protocol}

The processed dataset has the following contract:

\begin{lstlisting}
inputs:  (1530, 27, 6000), float32
labels:  (1530,), int64
classes: 17
layout:  samples x sensors x time samples
\end{lstlisting}

All three experiments used the same seed-42 setup allocation:

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Identical grouped split used by all three pipelines.}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrl@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Split} & \textbf{Segments} & \textbf{Recordings} & \textbf{Setup IDs} \\
\midrule
Train      & 1,020 & 102 & 3, 0, 7, 2, 4, 6 \\
Validation &   170 &  17 & 1 \\
Test       &   340 &  34 & 5, 8 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

All ten segments from one condition/setup recording remain in one split. Thus,
the sets of recording identifiers satisfy:

\[
R_{\mathrm{train}} \cap R_{\mathrm{validation}} = \varnothing,
\qquad
R_{\mathrm{train}} \cap R_{\mathrm{test}} = \varnothing,
\qquad
R_{\mathrm{validation}} \cap R_{\mathrm{test}} = \varnothing.
\]

Every class contributes 60 train, 10 validation, and 20 test segments. Because
the test set is class-balanced, macro recall is numerically equal to accuracy
in this experiment. It should not be interpreted as independent confirmation
of the accuracy result.

All three pipelines apply per-sensor Z-score normalization:

\[
x'_{i,s,t}
=
\frac{x_{i,s,t}-\mu_{s,\mathrm{train}}}
     {\sigma_{s,\mathrm{train}}}.
\]

Only training samples are used to calculate \(\mu_{s,\mathrm{train}}\) and
\(\sigma_{s,\mathrm{train}}\). The resulting statistics are then applied to
train, validation, and test data, avoiding preprocessing leakage.

The core Conv1D--ResNet--LSTM structure is preserved. However, the proposed
pipeline is not a one-variable modification. It changes the input semantics,
LSTM gate activation, optimizer, regularization, batch size, model-selection
criterion, data loader, and distribution strategy simultaneously. The measured
difference is therefore the effect of the complete pipeline package; it cannot
be attributed to any one component without an ablation study.

\subsection{Proposed LSTM versus tsai ResNet}

The proposed LSTM and tsai experiments share the same dataset, setup IDs,
per-sensor normalization, batch size, and reported metrics. Their input tensors
use different framework conventions but represent the same physical axes:

\begin{lstlisting}
Keras LSTM: (batch, time, sensors) = (N, 6000, 27)
tsai ResNet: (batch, channels, time) = (N, 27, 6000)
\end{lstlisting}

Thus, unlike the teacher-reference layout, both models treat 6,000 as the time
dimension. Their training configurations are summarized below.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Configuration of the proposed LSTM and tsai ResNet.}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{3.5cm}XX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Component} & \textbf{Proposed LSTM} & \textbf{tsai ResNet} \\
\midrule
Framework & TensorFlow/Keras & PyTorch, fastai, and tsai \\
Model & 1DCNN--LSTM--ResNet & One-dimensional ResNet \\
Input convention & \texttt{(N, 6000, 27)} & \texttt{(N, 27, 6000)} \\
Parameters & 508,689 & 493,713 \\
Batch size & 32 & 32 \\
Optimizer & AdamW & Adam with one-cycle schedule \\
Regularization & Dropout, L2, weight decay & Weight decay \(10^{-4}\) \\
Epoch control & Maximum 100; early stopping & Fixed 20 epochs \\
Model restoration & Minimum validation loss & No best-epoch restoration \\
Recorded precision & Float32 & Float32 \\
Training devices & Two T4 replicas & One T4, \texttt{cuda:0} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The tsai ResNet contains no recurrent layer. Its temporal convolutions can be
computed in parallel across the signal, whereas the LSTM state at step \(t\)
depends on the state at step \(t-1\). This architectural distinction is central
to the speed comparison.

\subsection{Change in temporal representation}

The teacher-reference flow is:

\begin{lstlisting}
(batch, 27, 6000)
        |
        v
stride-2 Conv1D
        |
        v
(batch, 14, 64)
        |
        v
ResNet and LSTM over approximately 14 steps
\end{lstlisting}

The proposed flow is:

\begin{lstlisting}
(batch, 6000, 27)
        |
        v
stride-2 Conv1D
        |
        v
(batch, 3000, 64)
        |
        v
ResNet and LSTM over 3000 steps
\end{lstlisting}

The proposed layout is more consistent with the physical meaning of the data:
the recurrent dimension follows time, while the 27 simultaneous sensor values
form the feature vector at each time step. The cost is a much longer recurrent
sequence.

\subsection{Change in capacity and regularization}

The proposed model reduced the parameter count by:

\[
1 - \frac{508{,}689}{3{,}949{,}137}
= 87.12\%.
\]

Despite containing fewer parameters, it performs more sequential work because
the LSTM sequence grows from approximately 14 to 3,000 steps. Dropout, L2, and
AdamW further distinguish it from the unregularized teacher-reference model.
These mechanisms are expected to lower training accuracy while potentially
improving validation and test performance.

The proposed pipeline correctly classified 204 of 340 test samples, compared
with 191 for the teacher-reference pipeline. This is a gain of 13 correct
predictions. The accuracy and precision improvements are meaningful enough to
motivate further experiments, but the smaller macro-F1 gain shows that the
benefit is not evenly distributed among classes.

Compared directly with the proposed LSTM, tsai increased validation accuracy
by 4.71 percentage points and validation macro F1 by 5.28 percentage points.
On test data, it increased accuracy by only 0.29 percentage points, equivalent
to one additional correct sample, while test macro F1 increased by 2.65
percentage points. Its test macro precision was 2.12 percentage points lower.
Consequently, the two new models have effectively tied test accuracy, while
tsai provides the better macro F1 in this run.

\subsection{Overfitting}

For accuracy, the train--test gaps are:

\[
G_{\mathrm{teacher}}
= 100.00 - 56.18
= 43.82 \text{ percentage points},
\]

\[
G_{\mathrm{proposed}}
= 93.14 - 60.00
= 33.14 \text{ percentage points}.
\]

For tsai:

\[
G_{\mathrm{tsai}}
= 100.00 - 60.29
= 39.71 \text{ percentage points}.
\]

The proposed pipeline reduces the gap by 10.68 percentage points. The
teacher-reference model reached approximately 100\% training accuracy by
epoch 10 while validation accuracy remained near 50--55\%, which is strong
evidence of memorization. The proposed model still overfits: training loss
continues to decrease after validation loss reaches its minimum, and validation
accuracy fluctuates considerably. Nevertheless, its lower training score and
higher test score indicate that the regularized pipeline generalizes better in
this run.

The tsai model also reached 100\% train accuracy. Its 39.71-point train--test
gap is smaller than the teacher-reference gap but 6.57 points larger than the
proposed LSTM gap. Thus, tsai obtains the highest validation metrics and macro
F1, but the proposed LSTM shows the smallest accuracy-based overfitting gap.

\subsection{Model-selection difference}

The proposed run stopped after 21 epochs and restored the epoch selected by
minimum validation loss. Its minimum validation loss occurred near epoch 6.
The training log later reached a maximum validation accuracy of 67.06\% at
epoch 17, but those weights were not selected because validation loss had
worsened.

The teacher-reference run monitored validation accuracy, reached its best
value of 54.71\% near epoch 22, and stopped after 52 epochs. Thus, final model
selection is not controlled between the two LSTM experiments. The proposed model's
final validation accuracy of 58.82\% must not be confused with the transient
67.06\% observed during training. A fair architecture comparison should use
the same monitored metric and patience in both LSTM pipelines. For a class-balanced
classification problem, validation macro F1 is also a reasonable selection
criterion.

The tsai run used a fixed 20-epoch one-cycle schedule without early stopping or
best-weight restoration. Its validation accuracy reached 72.35\% at epoch 12
but ended at 63.53\% after epoch 20. Therefore, all three pipelines use
different model-selection rules. The reported tsai result corresponds to the
last epoch, not the epoch with maximum validation accuracy.

Effective throughput (samples/s) & 1,308.42 & 122.19 & 126.69 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The proposed pipeline is approximately:

\[
\frac{8.3476}{0.7796} \approx 10.7
\]

times slower per steady-state epoch. Its complete run is approximately 2.88
times longer, despite stopping after fewer than half as many epochs.

This result does not contradict cuDNN optimization. The teacher-reference LSTM
processes only about 14 recurrent steps, whereas the proposed LSTM processes
3,000. The sequence-length ratio is approximately:

\[
\frac{3000}{14} \approx 214.
\]

The proposed model uses the more natural temporal orientation, but this choice
substantially increases computation. Moreover, its global batch of 32 is split
across two GPUs, leaving approximately 16 samples per replica. Gradient
all-reduce and model-replication overhead can reduce the benefit of the second
GPU at this dataset size. Therefore, the experimental result is that the new
pipeline is slower, not faster, than the teacher-reference adaptation.

The tsai ResNet is approximately 3.55\% faster per steady-state epoch than the
proposed LSTM and processes approximately 3.68\% more samples per second. Its
total run is 29.86 seconds shorter, although this comparison includes 20 fixed
tsai epochs versus 21 early-stopped LSTM epochs. More importantly, tsai obtains
this throughput on one T4 while the LSTM uses two replicas. Convolutional
operations can exploit parallelism across the complete time axis, whereas the
LSTM retains recurrent dependencies across 3,000 steps. Nevertheless, a pure
architecture-speed comparison should rerun both models on one GPU with the
same precision and batch size.

\section{Mixed-precision audit}

The beginning of the proposed notebook printed:

\begin{lstlisting}
Mixed precision policy: mixed_float16
\end{lstlisting}

However, its saved benchmark summary reported:

\begin{lstlisting}
precision_policy: float32
\end{lstlisting}

The notebook calls \texttt{clear\_session()} after setting the mixed-precision
policy and before constructing the model. In the installed Keras version,
\texttt{clear\_session()} resets global Keras state, including the global dtype
policy. The recorded result is therefore consistent with the model being built
under \texttt{float32}. This experiment must not be used as evidence that Tensor
Cores or mixed precision produced the measured result.

The intended order should be:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
tf.keras.backend.clear_session()
tf.keras.mixed_precision.set_global_policy(
    'mixed_float16' if gpus else 'float32'
)

with strategy.scope():
    model = build_model()

print('Policy after model creation:',
      tf.keras.mixed_precision.global_policy().name)
print('First Conv1D policy:', model.layers[1].dtype_policy)
\end{lstlisting}

The output classification layer should remain explicitly \texttt{float32} for
numerical stability. After correcting the order, the proposed pipeline must be
rerun before reporting a mixed-precision speedup.

\section{What can and cannot be concluded}

\subsection{Supported conclusions}

For the exact three runs examined here:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The proposed complete pipeline improves test accuracy by 3.82
          percentage points and macro F1 by 1.39 percentage points.
    \item It reduces the train--test accuracy gap by 10.68 percentage points.
    \item It uses 87.12\% fewer parameters.
    \item It is approximately 10.7 times slower per steady-state epoch.
    \item Its improvement is concentrated in a subset of classes rather than
          being uniform across all 17 classes.
    \item The tsai ResNet and proposed LSTM have nearly identical test
          accuracy: 60.29\% and 60.00\%, respectively.
    \item Tsai improves test macro F1 by 2.65 percentage points over the
          proposed LSTM, but has a 6.57-point larger train--test accuracy gap.
    \item Tsai is approximately 3.55\% faster per steady-state epoch while
          using one GPU instead of the LSTM's two replicas.
    \item Tsai and the LSTM make complementary class-level errors, especially
          for classes 12 and 16.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Unsupported causal claims}

The experiment does not establish that the accuracy gain was caused
specifically by sigmoid, AdamW, Dropout, the input transpose, or any other
single change. All of these components changed together. It also does not show
that mixed precision accelerated training, because the recorded policy was
\texttt{float32}. The tsai--LSTM comparison also does not isolate architecture
alone because the frameworks, optimizer schedules, device counts, and
model-selection procedures differ. Finally, one training seed and one grouped
split are insufficient for a strong claim of general superiority.

\section{Recommended follow-up experiments}

The following sequence would isolate the contribution of each modification:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Use the same grouped split, Z-score normalization, batch size, GPU
          count, precision, early-stopping metric, and patience for every run.
    \item Start from the teacher-reference pipeline and change only the input
          orientation from \texttt{(27, 6000)} to \texttt{(6000, 27)}.
    \item Change only the LSTM recurrent activation from softmax to sigmoid.
    \item Add Dropout, then L2, then AdamW in separate ablations.
    \item Evaluate one-GPU and two-GPU execution separately.
    \item Add identical early stopping and best-weight restoration to the tsai
          and proposed-LSTM experiments.
    \item Correct the mixed-precision initialization and compare float32 with
          mixed float16 under otherwise identical settings.
    \item Repeat each final configuration with at least five training seeds and
          report mean $\pm$ standard deviation.
    \item Evaluate multiple held-out setup combinations or grouped
          cross-validation to test sensitivity to setup IDs 5 and 8.
    \item Save per-sample predictions so that paired tests such as McNemar's
          test can assess whether the 13-sample teacher-to-LSTM improvement and
          one-sample tsai-to-LSTM difference are statistically reliable.
    \item Evaluate probability averaging or stacking between tsai and the LSTM
          because their errors on classes 12 and 16 are complementary.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

The proposed 1DCNN--LSTM--ResNet pipeline provides a more physically natural
time-series representation and improves generalization in the observed run.
Compared with the teacher-reference adaptation, it increases test accuracy
from 56.18\% to 60.00\%, increases macro F1 from 53.63\% to 55.02\%, and
reduces the train--test accuracy gap. These improvements are consistent with
the addition of regularization and the reduction in model capacity, but the
experiment does not isolate their individual effects.

The cost is substantial: the proposed model is approximately 10.7 times
slower per steady-state epoch because it processes a 3,000-step recurrent
sequence instead of an approximately 14-step sequence. Its gain also depends
heavily on classes 4 and 16, while performance deteriorates for classes 0, 3,
5, and 12 and remains zero for class 15. Therefore, the most defensible
conclusion is that the proposed pipeline offers a modest but uneven
generalization improvement at a large computational cost. Controlled
ablations, repeated runs, and a corrected mixed-precision experiment are
required before attributing the improvement to a specific design decision.

The tsai ResNet provides the strongest macro-F1 result, 57.67\%, and essentially
ties the proposed LSTM in test accuracy while using one GPU. It is therefore a
competitive and computationally simpler baseline for this dataset. However,
it reaches 100\% train accuracy, has a larger train--test gap than the proposed
LSTM, and completely misses classes 15 and 16. The current evidence supports
retaining both models for further study rather than declaring one universally
superior: tsai is preferable by macro F1 and single-GPU efficiency, while the
proposed LSTM exhibits less overfitting and uniquely recognizes class 16.
